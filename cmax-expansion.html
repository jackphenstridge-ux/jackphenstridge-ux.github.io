CMax Expansion

Making fragmented retail media feel like one system — told through three connected initiatives: visibility and trust, governance and activation, and cross-product interoperability.

Roughly 70% of retail media spend flowed through retailer-sold channels that were largely invisible in the product — brands were reconstructing performance by hand across separate retailer platforms. This program existed to close that gap.

The program in one sentence

CMax Expansion wasn't a single feature — it was a coordinated set of experiences that had to make fragmented retail media workflows feel coherent, while preserving retailer control, account permissions, existing operating models, and commercial requirements. My role spanned all three initiatives: translating commercial, technical, and retailer constraints into usable workflows, and creating shared interaction patterns for account linking, access states, and campaign context across the program.

The research

Research that shaped both the user experience and the business case

Research approach

We combined user, business, and competitive discovery across the ecosystem: agencies, brands, retailers, and internal commercial teams.

  • Internal workshops and persona research
  • User-journey mapping
  • Customer and retailer interviews
  • Prototype sessions with several major international retailers
  • Analysis of existing campaign workflows

What users told us

Retail media management was fragmented and operationally heavy. People juggled multiple accounts, tools, and reports, often falling back on spreadsheets just to understand performance.

They wanted:

  • A holistic view across brands, retailers, and campaigns
  • Easier access to key metrics and customizable reporting
  • Proactive alerts
  • The ability to make adjustments without hopping between screens

These needs became the blueprint for Unified Insights, account consolidation, retailer-billed workflows, and automated optimization.

The key constraint

Agencies and brands don't automatically value a multi-retailer view.

Budgets are planned retailer by retailer, so without clear cross-retailer insight, there's little incentive to change established workflows.

Larger brands often staff a dedicated team per retailer. Smaller and long-tail advertisers don't have that headcount and need something simpler and more scalable.

That made reducing operational effort while making value visible the central design problem, not a secondary feature.

The commercial opportunity

The goal was to shift CMax from a tool people used only when buying media through Criteo, to a platform brands and agencies choose to run more of their retail media business through. A large majority of customers weren't buying CMax explicitly, which pointed to real headroom.

The takeaway wasn't "add more features." It was making CMax easier to adopt, easier to operate, and more valuable across the full retail media lifecycle.

That spanned planning and activation through optimization and reporting.

01 · Visibility & trust

Unified Insights

Designing trust into account linking and unified campaign visibility.

The problem

Brands and agencies managing retail media across multiple retailers were working in a fragmented system: retailer-specific platforms on one side, our product on the other. There was no single place to see internally-billed and retailer-sold campaigns side by side, and roughly 70% of retail media spend sat on the invisible side of that line. Third-party partners had no programmatic access to retailer-sold campaign data, and many managed-service brands had no direct access to those campaigns at all.

The fragmentation wasn't just a UI problem. The relationship between accounts, permissions, consent, billing, and campaign ownership was genuinely hard to explain: one user could see a campaign without permission to edit it, while another could deep-link in and make changes.

The design question: how might we give users a unified view of their retail media activity while making access, consent, and edit boundaries clear?

Key decisions

Consent first, linking second — even though it added a step

The fastest path to "unified" would have surfaced connected campaigns as soon as a technical link existed. I pushed for a deliberate two-step sequence — consent, then linking — because campaign data appearing without a legible relationship would have undermined retailer trust in the whole model. The extra step was the trust mechanism, not friction.

Read-only as a product choice, not a limitation

With uneven permissions across users, we could have hidden restricted campaigns entirely, or shown everything and let edit attempts fail. I argued for a third path: make the surface honestly read-only for everyone, with deep links out for users who already held edit access elsewhere. Visibility improved without overpromising control.

Unified alongside, not blended in

Once linked, external campaigns appear automatically in the main campaign view — visually distinguished, not merged in. Users get the benefit of one surface without losing track of what they're looking at or who bills for it.

Treating terminology as information architecture

The project accumulated overlapping names for concepts that weren't actually synonyms. I drove the separation and naming work myself — it wasn't a wording pass at the end, it was part of designing the system.

Outcome

This initiative's broad rollout was deferred at launch — a related program shipped first with a narrower mechanism as the core focus. That deferral is part of the story, not a footnote: the account-linking and consent model designed here became the enabling mechanism for that later launch. The design infrastructure — the consent sequence, the linked-campaign visibility pattern, the read-only/deep-link permission model, and the terminology system — shipped into production through a different door than originally planned.

What I learned

A unified experience doesn't require every action to happen in one product. The most important design move wasn't adding functionality — it was giving users a coherent mental model of the relationship between products, then making the boundaries around visibility and control explicit. That principle became a recurring pattern across the whole program.

02 · Governance & activation

Authorized Buyers + Retailer Budget

Designing the activation path from retailer approval to campaign creation.

The problem

Retailer-sold activation required explicit approval, controlled access, and a usable execution path — none of which existed as a system. Without governance, any brand account with basic product access could potentially have launched retailer-sold campaigns without retailer approval, creating real risk around contracts, credit exposure, and eligibility.

The challenge was never just an approval screen. It was making the relationship between retailer control, brand access, campaign restrictions, balances, and campaign creation understandable across different account types and product surfaces.

How the pieces fit together

  1. A retailer approves a brand's account as an Authorized Buyer, at the account level.
  2. Campaign-type access is applied — the retailer controls which campaign and buy types are permitted.
  3. A retailer-funded balance becomes available for approved accounts to use.
  4. The brand creates a retailer-funded campaign using the standard campaign flow.
  5. The campaign appears in unified reporting with new activation and budget-model dimensions.

Key decisions

Tell the end-to-end story, not the permission story

Authorized status is presented as the start of an activation workflow, not a destination. Otherwise approval reads as bureaucracy instead of capability.

Make retailer control visible without making brands feel blocked

The brand side needed a mirror image of retailer control: clear visibility of what access exists, and predictable, explained states when something isn't permitted — rather than options that silently fail.

Use permissions to filter downstream choices

The retailers, balances, and campaign types shown during activation reflect the user's actual authorization state. Users never choose an option their account can't complete — the single biggest source of setup errors in permission-gated flows.

Design for the manual launch and the self-serve future at once

Launch had internal teams handling setup manually, with brands self-serving campaign creation. I shaped the terminology and structure so a future self-service model builds on the same mental model — phased rollout without a migration.

Outcome

This mechanism shipped to general availability as the core of the program. Retailers can approve eligible brand accounts, campaign access reflects retailer-defined permissions, and approved brands create retailer-funded campaigns through the standard flow.

What I learned

Permissioning isn't a standalone UX layer when it determines whether a user can complete the core job. The useful design work connected governance to the downstream action, so approval, access, funding, and campaign creation formed one understandable system — rather than "I designed an approval feature," the real story is connecting retailer governance to real campaign activation.

03 · Interoperability

Retailer Enablement

Designing interoperability without breaking an existing ecosystem.

The problem

A major retail partner wanted the benefits of the program, but not at the cost of disrupting the workflow its buyers already relied on in their own existing platform. They needed to retain visibility, configurability, and reporting in their established environment, while gaining unified visibility on our side. A full migration, requiring dual-edit capability that didn't exist yet, was off the table.

The design question: how might our product become the place where users understand and navigate their retail media activity, while the partner's environment remains the place where their execution happens?

Key decisions

Preserve the execution environment; add the visibility layer

Partner campaigns stayed editable in the partner's own environment. Our product provided visibility, aggregation, and a clear entry point into the relevant workflow. This is what made participation possible at all — the alternative was no adoption.

Account selection as the moment of commitment

When a user starts campaign creation, an account selector makes the execution destination explicit — our platform for native campaigns, or the linked partner account for partner-specific ones. The fork is a conscious choice, not a surprise three screens later.

Same-tab transitions with a guaranteed way home

Handing off to the partner's workflow in the same browser tab preserved continuity and avoided window sprawl; a persistent "back" affordance made the return path predictable. Small mechanics, but they carry the entire feeling of one coherent journey.

Interoperable, not artificially identical

The design deliberately avoided pretending the two products were one. Partner-specific branding signals the context switch. When products have different responsibilities and permissions, a legible boundary is more trustworthy than a cosmetic merge.

The best cross-product experience is not always a fully unified interface. A clear handoff can be more trustworthy than a superficial visual merge.

Outcome

This became the route by which the Unified Insights design infrastructure reached production. Partner-side buyers see linked campaigns in our product, start campaign creation with an explicit choice of execution environment, and keep partner-specific execution in the workflow they already trust — creating a real adoption path without demanding a platform migration.

What I learned

The design challenge was less about hiding the product boundary and more about making it understandable, navigable, and intentional.
