The programme

A cockpit is only as safe as the interface between pilot and system

I joined as HMI Engineer for avionics and mission systems on a test-and-evaluation programme for a new military rotorcraft. The job wasn't judging individual instruments or controls in isolation — it was assessing how the cockpit and mission systems performed as part of the whole aircraft, in the hands of the people who'd actually fly and operate it.

That meant working inside a genuinely cross-functional team: test pilots and aircrew who'd operate the systems, manufacturer subject-matter experts who built them, MoD stakeholders who owned the requirements and the risk, systems engineers, project management, and the ground and radio equipment operators who supported every trial from the ground.

The approach

Desk research, then ground trials, then the aircraft itself

The evaluation moved through distinct stages, each one de-risking the next: desk and document research first, to understand what the systems were meant to do and where the existing documentation left questions open; then ground-based trials and testing, working through interactions and edge cases before anything left the ground; then flight test itself, where workload and interaction assessment happened in the environment it actually mattered in.

Rather than being handed a finished test plan, I worked directly alongside test pilots and aircrew to design the test plans themselves. An interface can't really be judged from a document — it has to be judged against what the people using it actually need under real operational conditions, so getting close to that operational judgment early mattered more than any single test script.

The challenges

Safety-critical, multi-agency, and thin on documentation

Safety-critical constraints

Every change to test scope or method had to hold up against strict safety-critical guidelines — there was no room for a "we'll fix it later" iteration cycle.

Translating operational language

Turning what aircrew described operationally into concrete, testable interface criteria was a constant translation exercise between two genuinely different vocabularies.

Multi-agency coordination

Planning trials and tests meant coordinating across multiple agencies, bodies, and operators at once — scheduling, sign-off, and logistics were as much a design problem as the interface itself.

Sparse manufacturer documentation

Accessible documentation from the manufacturer was often limited, which pushed more of the discovery work into direct, hands-on investigation rather than desk research alone.

The outcome

Findings that shaped both the aircraft's sign-off and its future design

The evaluation's findings fed directly into the aircraft's sign-off. I co-authored a report delivered to MoD senior leadership, advising on the best operational safety window for the aircraft and its equipment — a judgment grounded in what the trials had actually shown about how the systems and their operators worked together.

Separately, the work also produced recommendations back to the manufacturer, aimed at improving how future aircraft systems were designed and implemented — feedback intended to shape not just this programme, but the ones that followed it.

Reflection

The human factor doesn't show up in a spec sheet

This was the project that pushed me toward design in the first place. Working this closely with the people actually operating safety-critical systems made it clear that the hardest and most important problems weren't in the systems themselves — they were in the interface between the system and the person relying on it under pressure. That's the thread that runs through everything I've worked on since.
